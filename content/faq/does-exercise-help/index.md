## An acute lift, and how it was established

Reed and Ones pooled studies of single bouts of aerobic exercise and found that one session is associated with a short-term increase in positive activated affect: the energised end of mood, feeling alert and awake, rather than the calm end. A meta-analysis is the right design for asking whether an effect turns up consistently across many small samples. It is a weak design for asking how long the effect lasts in any one person, or which people it fails for.

Two limits belong with the finding. The effect is acute and fades, so it describes the hours after a session and not the weeks around it. And this site does not state the pooled effect size or the number of studies behind it, because the source abstract could not be re-verified — a gap in our checking, not a hidden number.

## Organised dance is a different question

Koch and colleagues meta-analysed 41 controlled intervention studies of dance movement therapy and dance, and found a medium overall effect on health-related psychological outcomes (d = 0.60). That is a stronger design than a single trial, because it compares programmes against control conditions across many settings.

Three qualifications travel with the number. Heterogeneity between studies was high, which means the true effect is unlikely to be the same size everywhere. Effects were larger when outcomes came from observational measures rather than self-report, and that pattern may indicate bias in how outcomes were rated. And the trials studied structured programmes, many of them in healthcare or preventive settings, with a facilitator and a schedule. They are not evidence about a night of dancing for its own sake, which nobody in these studies was doing.

## What the lift is not

An hour of running can change how a difficulty feels by the end of it. It does not, on its own, change the difficulty. This site keeps the two apart: relief changes the felt state, resolution changes the conditions that produce it. Relief has value of its own, and treating it as worthless is its own mistake.

The reason to keep them apart is that relief can be repeated. Hayes and colleagues describe experiential avoidance, the pattern in which a person works to escape or alter unwanted internal experience and, in doing so, keeps arranging their life around it. A run that reliably makes the afternoon bearable can become the reason the conversation is never had. That is a synthesis drawn from separate lines of research, not a finding from a study that tested the loop directly, and it may not hold for particular people or particular activities.

## What would settle it

Studies that follow people for months, measure both mood and the circumstances producing the strain, and compare exercise against another active strategy would settle more than the acute literature can. That work is thin, so for the question of whether exercise changes what a person is dealing with there is insufficient evidence either way. For the question of whether it lifts mood for a few hours, the answer is a fairly confident yes.
