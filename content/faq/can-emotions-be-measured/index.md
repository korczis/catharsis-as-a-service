## What an instrument actually records

Nothing in a laboratory records an emotion. A camera records the geometry of a face over time, and a coder or an algorithm labels the movements. Electrodes record the interval between heartbeats, or the conductance of the skin on a fingertip. A questionnaire records what a person is willing and able to say about their state. The emotion is a construct inferred from one or more of those recordings, and the quality of the inference is the whole question.

Mauss and Robinson reviewed the field and concluded that there is no gold-standard measure: experiential, physiological and behavioural measures converge only modestly and cannot be treated as interchangeable. That is a narrative review rather than a pooled estimate, so it summarises a literature rather than quantifying it, but the conclusion has not been overturned by newer multimodal methods.

## Faces

Barrett and colleagues reviewed studies of how people move their faces during emotion and how observers read those movements. Typical configurations do co-occur with the expected emotions more often than chance, but weakly and inconsistently: the same configuration accompanies different states, the same state is shown in many ways, and the pattern varies across people, situations and cultures. The review is associated with one theoretical position, and some researchers argue the expression–emotion link is stronger than it concludes.

Durán and Fernández-Dols put numbers on it in a meta-analysis. For six classic basic emotions, average co-occurrence effect sizes were .13 for full expressions coded with FACS and .23 once partial expressions were included, and reported intensity correlated with expressed intensity at .30 on average. Heterogeneity between samples was found, and co-occurrence was highest for disgust and lowest for surprise, so these averages do not describe every emotion equally.

## Bodies

Siegel and colleagues meta-analysed 202 studies of autonomic responses and found that they did not clearly distinguish one emotion category from another, with substantial variation within categories. Kreibig's earlier review of 134 publications is a partial counterpoint, reporting considerable specificity once emotion subtypes are considered. The two syntheses disagree, so the defensible statement is that no consistent autonomic fingerprint has been established — not that physiology is uninformative.

Coherence across systems is likewise partial. In Mauss and colleagues' film study, inducing amusement and sadness, experience and facial behaviour were highly associated while physiology was associated only modestly. Each kind of measure carries variance the others do not. That is one laboratory study, and coherence may differ by emotion and intensity, as the gap between amusement and sadness already shows.

## What follows for anyone reading a number

Heart rate variability can index cardiac vagal tone, and electrodermal activity is a usable signal, but both are easily misconstrued without careful design, analysis and reporting — which is what Laborde and colleagues and Boucsein's methods guidance exist to enforce. That guidance concerns how to measure well. It is not evidence that any signal corresponds to a specific emotion.

So the workable answer: aspects of emotional responding can be measured, one system at a time, with known error. A single reading from a single sensor supports a conclusion about one person in one moment only with low confidence, and a product claiming otherwise is claiming more than the literature supports.
