## Moving in time with someone changes how you regard them

The cleanest evidence comes from experiments that strip dancing down to almost nothing. Hove and Risen had participants tap in or out of time with an experimenter; Wiltermuth and Heath had groups walk or sing in step. Synchrony increased liking for a partner and increased later cooperation, including cooperation that cost the participant something personally. Because the synchrony was assigned rather than chosen, these are experiments, not correlations: the direction of the effect is not in doubt.

What they do not settle is size and reach. Tapping a finger to a metronome is minimal compared with a room of people dancing, the groups were small, and the outcomes were measured shortly afterwards. Rennung and Göritz pooled 60 such experiments and found a medium effect of interpersonal synchrony on prosocial attitudes and behaviour — with the effect moderated by experimenter effects and by intentionality, which means part of it depends on how the studies were run. That is a real caution, not a footnote.

## Synchrony and effort contribute separately

Tarr and colleagues ran a group-dance experiment with a 2 × 2 design, varying synchrony and physical exertion independently. Each raised pain thresholds, an indirect proxy for endorphin activity, and each increased in-group bonding. The design matters: it shows the two ingredients are not the same thing, and that a hard, unsynchronised session and an easy, synchronised one each do something.

The proxy matters too. Endorphin release was not measured directly — a raised pain threshold is consistent with it and does not single it out. This is one experiment, and one experiment needs replication before the mechanism is treated as established.

## The music is doing its own work

Salimpoor and colleagues studied listeners with music they had chosen themselves, using PET and fMRI. Peak emotional responses — the shiver at a particular moment — were accompanied by striatal dopamine release, with activity in the caudate more involved in the anticipation and the nucleus accumbens in the peak itself. Anticipation and arrival register differently, which is an appealing match for how a build-up and a drop are constructed.

The match is an analogy, not a finding. Electronic dance music was not studied, nor were crowds; the listeners were alone in a scanner with headphones. A small imaging study establishes that something is going on in reward-related circuitry during musical peaks. It does not license a story about what a dancefloor does.

## What the whole picture leaves open

Put together, the evidence says the pleasure of dancing with others is not mysterious: synchrony, exertion and music each contribute something that can be measured. Every one of those measurements was taken during the activity or minutes to hours after it. Whether any of it reaches the next morning is a separate question, and the studies cited here were not designed to answer it.
