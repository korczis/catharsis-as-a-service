## Two different changes

*Relief* is a change in how a state feels. *Resolution* is a change in the conditions that produce the state. From the inside they are hard to tell apart, because in both cases the person feels better afterwards. A long run, a good cry, an evening of loud music and a difficult conversation can all end in relief. Only some of them also end in resolution.

Saying so is not a verdict against relief. Recovering from an activated state is valuable on its own terms, and a person who is calmer is usually better placed to deal with whatever is waiting. The claim is only that the feeling does not report on the situation.

## Why relief tends to repeat itself

Learning theory supplies the first half of the reason. A behaviour that removes an aversive state is negatively reinforced: it becomes more likely to happen again in similar circumstances. That is adaptive when the behaviour also addresses the source. When it does not, each repetition is still rewarded, and the source is still there.

Hayes and colleagues (1996) described *experiential avoidance*, the attempt to escape or suppress unwanted internal experience even at long-term cost, and argued that it is a functional process shared across many psychological difficulties. Applying that account to a night out or a collective release is an inference rather than a tested result, and not every cathartic experience is avoidance. It does suggest a question worth asking: is the state being worked through, or postponed?

## Why relief is remembered generously

The second half of the reason is memory. Fredrickson and Kahneman (1993) found that retrospective evaluations of an experience are dominated by its most intense moment and by how it ends, while its duration is largely neglected. In the best-known demonstration, most participants chose to repeat a longer episode of cold-water discomfort that ended slightly less painfully over a shorter one that did not (Kahneman et al., 1993).

Applied to a restorative evening, the rule predicts that a strong peak and a good ending will make the whole episode remembered as more helpful than its lasting effect justifies, which makes it attractive to repeat. That application is an extrapolation from short laboratory episodes, and the pattern is not universal: Rode and colleagues (2007) found, across three studies of remembered meals, consistent duration neglect but no peak, primacy or recency effects. One domain of pleasant experience, not a test of painful ones, but enough to show that the rule has limits.

## The useful question afterwards

None of this recommends giving up what brings relief. It recommends a second question alongside "did it help?" — namely, "what exactly changed?"

If the answer is how you feel, that is relief, and it counts. If the answer also includes what you now understand, what you have decided, or what you will do differently, something has begun to resolve. The two can coexist in the same evening; they just need to be counted separately.

No study cited here tested this whole loop directly. It is a synthesis of separate lines of research, and for particular people or particular activities it may not hold.
