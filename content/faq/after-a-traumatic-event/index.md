## What the debriefing review actually found

A Cochrane systematic review of eleven randomised trials examined single-session individual psychological debriefing delivered within a month of a traumatic event. It found no evidence that the intervention prevents PTSD or reduces distress, found some evidence of increased PTSD risk at one year in one trial, and advised that compulsory debriefing should cease.

Two limits belong with that result. The trials were generally of poor quality. And the review evaluated one specific format: a single, structured, individual session, often offered to everyone exposed to an event whether or not they had symptoms. It does not show that talking after trauma is harmful in general, and it did not evaluate other early interventions.

The practical reading is narrow and worth stating plainly. Being required to recount an event, to a stranger, on a schedule set by someone else, has not been shown to help and may not be neutral. That says nothing about a conversation you choose, with someone you trust, when you want it.

## What the trial evidence supports instead

The picture changes once symptoms are in the frame. Early multiple-session interventions showed no clinically important benefit over usual care for trauma-exposed people who were not screened for symptoms. For people who did have traumatic stress symptoms, trauma-focused CBT, cognitive therapy without exposure and EMDR showed clinically important benefits. For chronic PTSD, trauma-focused CBT and EMDR reduced clinician-assessed symptoms more than waitlist or usual care.

These are group-level results with real limits. The Cochrane review rated the evidence for every comparison as very low quality, drop-out was higher in the active treatments, and long-term follow-up is limited. Choosing among these treatments requires clinical assessment, which no page can do.

The shape that emerges is a distinction between offering something to everyone who was present and offering something to people who are struggling. Screening, waiting, and then treating those with symptoms is supported better than treating in advance.

## Why "just get it out" is not the mechanism

Emotional processing theory holds that lasting change in fear requires activation of the fear memory together with information incompatible with it; activation alone is not sufficient. The theory accounts for why retelling can help in one setting and do nothing in another: the retelling is a vehicle for the new information, not the active ingredient on its own. Later research has revised parts of the theory, and the effectiveness of treatments rests on trials rather than on the theory itself.

This is the same distinction the rest of the site draws. Recounting an event can produce release. Whether anything changes depends on what the recounting is attached to.

## In practice

There is no deadline. Not talking in the first days is not a failure, and talking is not a treatment you owe anyone. What the evidence does support is watching what happens over the following weeks, and treating persistence as the signal rather than intensity in the first hours.

If distress persists, disrupts daily life, or includes thoughts of harming yourself, contact a qualified professional; immediate danger warrants contacting local emergency services.
