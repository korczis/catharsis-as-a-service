## What the large synthesis found

Pizarro and colleagues pooled 50 samples covering participants in demonstrations, rituals, ceremonies and celebrations (N = 182,738). Collective effervescence during these gatherings was associated with self-transcendent emotions, group identity, social integration, wellbeing and collective efficacy. That is a large body of people and a consistent pattern, and it is worth taking seriously.

It is also mostly correlational. The pooled studies do not show that gatherings cause these outcomes: people who already feel connected to a group are more likely to attend, more likely to report intensity, and more likely to report wellbeing for reasons that have nothing to do with the night itself. The association is real; the arrow is open.

## Why the experiments cannot close the gap

The experimental work — tapping and walking in synchrony, group dance, listening to self-chosen music — is where causal direction is established, because the conditions were assigned. But those studies measured liking, cooperation, pain thresholds, closeness and reward responses during the activity or shortly after it. Nobody followed participants for a week.

So on the specific question of whether collective rhythm reduces the difficulties people bring into it, there is insufficient evidence. That is not the same as evidence of absence. Longer studies could find lasting benefits; they have largely not been run, and the honest position is that the question stands open rather than answered negatively.

## The one mechanism with a longer horizon

There is a plausible route by which a night out could matter later, and it is social rather than physiological. Cohen and Wills reviewed the literature on social support and found that the perceived availability of support matched to the needs a stressor creates is associated with protection against its adverse effects, while social integration has a broader association with wellbeing.

Read carefully, this points away from the dancefloor and towards the people on it. What would plausibly carry over is not the effervescence but a relationship that is still there on Tuesday. The evidence here is largely observational and cannot fully separate cause from effect, so this is a direction worth acting on, not a demonstrated mechanism.

## Relief and resolution, again

A crowd reliably produces relief: the felt state changes, sometimes dramatically. Resolution means the conditions producing the strain have changed. Relief has value of its own and does not need to be justified by anything further. But relief can be repeated indefinitely — the next event is already on sale — while the circumstances stay exactly as they were. That argument is the project's synthesis of separate research lines, not a finding from a study that tested the loop directly.

The practical version is unglamorous. If a night out leaves you closer to two people you can call, something may have changed. If it leaves you with a good memory and the same Monday, that is still worth having, and it is not the same thing.
