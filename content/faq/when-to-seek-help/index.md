## The plain answer first

If you are in immediate danger, contact local emergency services now. That is not a threshold to deliberate over.

Short of that, distress that persists, disrupts daily life, or includes thoughts of harming yourself warrants contact with a qualified professional. This is standard safety guidance rather than a research finding, and it is deliberately general, because the thresholds at which services intervene and the services that exist at all differ by country. Looking up what is available where you live, before you need it, is worth the ten minutes.

## Why duration beats intensity as a signal

People tend to judge whether something is serious by how bad the worst hour felt. That is an unreliable instrument. Very intense distress can pass within a day, and distress that never reaches a peak can quietly reorganise a life over months.

Two better questions are how long it has gone on and what it has cost. If sleep, eating, work, or contact with other people have changed and stayed changed, that is information independent of how the feeling ranks on a scale. So is finding that things which used to provide relief no longer do.

None of this is a test, and none of it produces a verdict. It is a way of noticing that you already have enough grounds to ask, rather than waiting for a feeling dramatic enough to justify asking.

## What the research adds about repetitive thinking

Among the emotion-regulation strategies studied, rumination shows the largest association with symptoms of anxiety, depression, eating and substance-related disorders, and it predicts the onset of depression. The pooled data are largely correlational and cannot show that rumination causes symptoms, so this is a reason to take repetitive brooding seriously as a marker, not evidence that stopping it would resolve anything on its own.

The trauma literature points the same way. Across 75 studies, posttraumatic stress symptoms are associated with rumination (r = .52), experiential avoidance (r = .48) and thought suppression (r = .29). These are associations rather than causes, and the few longitudinal studies suggested somewhat smaller associations.

Read together, they suggest a pattern worth watching in yourself: going over the same ground repeatedly without moving, and steering around the situation, the place or the person rather than through it. Neither pattern assesses anyone, and neither means something is wrong. They are reasons to raise it with someone who can ask follow-up questions.

## What asking actually involves

A first conversation with a general practitioner, a psychologist or a local service is usually a description of what has changed and for how long. You do not need to have a name for it, and you do not need to have tried self-help first. If you are unsure whether it counts as serious enough, that uncertainty is a reason to ask rather than a reason to wait.

The content of this site is general education, not medical advice. It cannot assess your situation, and nothing on it replaces that conversation.
