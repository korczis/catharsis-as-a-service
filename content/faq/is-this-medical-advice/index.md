## The short version

No. The research notes and advice on this site are general education based on published research. They are not medical advice, they cannot assess an individual situation, and they do not replace assessment or treatment by a qualified professional. That is a statement about the purpose of the site, not an empirical finding, and it applies to every page without exception.

## What general education can and cannot do

The library reports what studies observed, in whom, with which design, and what the results cannot establish. Almost all of it is group-level: an average difference between conditions, an association across a sample, a pooled effect across trials. A group average describes a distribution, not the next person to read it. People inside those samples differed widely from one another, and a person outside them may differ more.

So a finding can tell you what tended to happen to people studied under particular conditions. It cannot tell you what is happening to you, what caused it, or what would help. Those are judgements that depend on history, circumstances, physical health and the things nobody wrote down, and they are made by a professional who can ask questions and follow up.

This is why the advice entries are written as practices to try rather than as prescriptions, why each carries explicit limits, and why none of them promises an outcome. Where the evidence is thin, the pages say so, because "insufficient evidence" is a finding about the literature rather than a verdict on your experience.

## What the site does not do

Nothing here measures, profiles or assesses you. The artwork's telemetry, its status line and its charts are artistic values chosen to express a relationship; the interactive model on the methods page runs on invented inputs and says so. No page infers anything about a visitor, and no number displayed anywhere is a reading of a person.

The site also does not recommend, rank or endorse any particular treatment, provider, medication or service. Where research on a treatment is cited, it is cited as evidence about a group of trials, with the quality of those trials stated.

## When to involve someone qualified

Distress that persists, disrupts daily life, or includes thoughts of harming yourself warrants contact with a qualified professional. Immediate danger warrants contacting local emergency services. This is standard safety guidance rather than a finding, and the details matter locally: the thresholds at which services intervene, and the services that exist at all, differ by country.

Reading is not a substitute for that step, and it is not a test you have to pass before taking it. If you are unsure whether what you are experiencing is serious enough, that uncertainty is itself a good reason to ask someone qualified rather than to keep reading.
