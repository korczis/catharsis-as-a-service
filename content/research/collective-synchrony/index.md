## Why crowds move together

A dancing crowd entrains to a shared beat. People adjust their movements to the music and to each other until hundreds of bodies move at nearly the same tempo. Research on interpersonal synchrony asks what that coordination does to the people involved.

## Synchrony, liking and cooperation

Hove and Risen (2009) asked participants to tap along to a visual metronome while an experimenter tapped beside them. When the two tapped in synchrony, participants later rated the experimenter as more likeable than when their tapping was out of time, even though nothing else in the interaction differed.

Wiltermuth and Heath (2009) had small groups walk in step, sing or move together, or do the same activities out of synchrony. Groups that had acted in synchrony cooperated more in subsequent economic games, including games in which cooperation required individuals to give up some personal gain. The authors propose that synchrony strengthens the sense of being part of a group.

## Dance, exertion and pain thresholds

Tarr, Launay, Cohen and Dunbar (2015) manipulated two variables in group dance: whether participants moved in synchrony and how physically demanding the movements were. Both synchrony and exertion independently raised pain thresholds, and synchrony also increased reported closeness to the rest of the group.

Pain threshold is used in this research as an indirect marker of endogenous opioid activity, because endorphins have analgesic effects. Opioid release itself was not measured. The findings support the interpretation that group dance works partly as a bonding mechanism, while leaving the physiological pathway to be confirmed with direct measures.

## Music, anticipation and reward

Salimpoor and colleagues (2011) combined positron emission tomography and functional MRI while listeners heard music they had chosen because it reliably moved them. Moments of peak emotional response were accompanied by dopamine release in the striatum. Activity in the caudate was associated with anticipating those moments and activity in the nucleus accumbens with experiencing them. The structure of a build-up and a drop in electronic dance music is an obvious cultural parallel, although that genre was not the subject of the study.

Janata, Tomic and Haberman (2012) studied *groove*, the pleasurable urge to move in time with music, and linked it to sensorimotor coupling between perception of rhythm and bodily movement.

## What these findings explain

Taken together, the studies describe why shared rhythmic activity feels good and brings people closer. Coordinated movement strengthens affiliation, exertion and synchrony are associated with analgesic and bonding effects, and musical peaks engage reward circuitry. They map closely onto the *synchronization* and *discharge* stages of the artifact's process.

## What they do not show

The outcomes measured are liking, cooperation, pain thresholds, reported closeness and reward responses during or shortly after the activity. None of the studies measured whether personal difficulties that participants brought with them were reduced afterwards. Collective rhythm is a genuine social and physiological resource. The artifact's question is not whether it works, but what people expect it to accomplish.
