## A word with three careers

The Greek *katharsis* meant cleansing. In medical writing it described purging the body, and in religious practice ritual purification. Aristotle gave the word its most influential aesthetic sense: in the *Poetics* he describes tragedy as achieving, through pity and fear, the katharsis of such emotions (1449b).

Aristotle did not define the term further, and commentators have argued for centuries about what he meant. Three readings recur. *Purgation* treats emotions as something expelled. *Purification* treats them as refined into a better form. *Clarification* treats catharsis as a kind of understanding reached through the drama. The disagreement is not only philological. It already contains the question this project is built around: does release change something, or does it only relieve?

## The clinical version

In 1895 Josef Breuer and Sigmund Freud published *Studies on Hysteria*. They described a cathartic method in which patients recalled distressing memories, often under hypnosis, and expressed the emotion attached to them. They called the discharge *abreaction*. Breuer's patient, known as Anna O., called the treatment the "talking cure".

Freud later abandoned hypnosis and the cathartic method in favour of free association and the analysis of resistance. One reason he gave was that relief after abreaction was frequently temporary: symptoms returned when the underlying conflict had not been worked through. The method faded in clinical practice, but its hydraulic picture survived in popular culture. Emotions build pressure, and pressure has to be let out.

## The empirical version

Modern psychology turned catharsis into a claim that can be tested: does expressing an emotion reduce it?

For anger, controlled experiments found the opposite of what the hydraulic model predicts. Venting anger while focusing on its cause kept the anger active and was followed by more aggression, not less ([Research note 02](@/research/venting-hypothesis/index.md)).

For distressing experiences more broadly, the picture is more nuanced. In studies of expressive writing, participants who wrote about both the facts and their feelings about a traumatic event later showed health benefits compared with control groups (Pennebaker & Beall, 1986). Subsequent analyses suggested that benefit was linked to building a coherent account of the experience, that is, to cognitive processing, rather than to emotional discharge alone (Pennebaker, 1997).

Research on emotion regulation reframed the question again. Instead of a binary choice between release and suppression, it asks which strategies change the course of an emotion, at what point in its unfolding, and with what costs (Gross, 1998).

## A working definition

In this project, *catharsis* means a subjectively felt release of emotional tension that follows expression, physical exertion or immersion in an experience. The definition deliberately says nothing about whether the cause of that tension has changed. Whether it has is the empirical question, and the answer differs by emotion, by method and by context.

## Why the distinction matters

When relief is mistaken for resolution, people repeat whatever made them feel better and are surprised when the underlying difficulty returns. The artifact's status line, `problem_solved: false`, is a compact way of keeping those two outcomes apart. The following notes examine each part of that claim: anger and venting, collective rhythm, relief and reinforcement, and the limits of measuring emotion at all.
