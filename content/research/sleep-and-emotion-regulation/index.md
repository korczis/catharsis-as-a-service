## Why sleep belongs in a library about catharsis

Emotional strain and sleep are entangled in ordinary experience. After an argument, a loss or a stressful day, people lie awake replaying events, and the next day everything feels harder. A library about release and regulation might seem to have little to say about sleep, because sleep is not something a person does to an emotion. But that is precisely why it is useful: sleep changes the conditions under which every other regulation strategy operates, and, unlike most of those strategies, it can be manipulated in controlled experiments with healthy volunteers.

This note separates three kinds of evidence. Experiments that deprive or restrict sleep show what sleep loss does to emotional functioning over a day or a few days. Longitudinal studies show how poor sleep relates to later mental health over months and years. Trials of sleep interventions provide an indirect test of whether improving sleep improves mental health. Each answers a different question.

## Terms: deprivation, restriction and fragmentation

[Sleep deprivation](../../glossary/#sleep-deprivation) in research usually means *total* sleep deprivation: staying awake through a night under supervision. *Partial sleep restriction* shortens sleep, for example to a few hours a night for several nights. *Sleep fragmentation* interrupts sleep with repeated awakenings. These manipulations resemble different real-life situations: a night shift, a week of short nights before a deadline, or broken sleep while caring for an infant. They are not the same as insomnia, which is a persistent difficulty falling or staying asleep, and they do not model what happens over years.

## What the experiments show

Palmer and colleagues (2024) conducted a preregistered systematic review and meta-analysis of more than fifty years of experimental research. They included studies that experimentally reduced sleep via total sleep deprivation, partial restriction or fragmentation in healthy populations and measured positive affect, negative affect, general mood disturbance, emotional reactivity, anxiety symptoms or depressive symptoms. The analysis covered 1,338 effect sizes across 154 studies with 5,717 participants aged 7 to 79.

All forms of sleep loss reduced positive affect, with standardised mean differences between -0.27 and -1.14 depending on the form of sleep loss. All forms increased anxiety symptoms (SMD 0.57 to 0.63). And all forms blunted arousal in response to emotional stimuli (SMD -0.20 to -0.53). Findings for negative affect, for ratings of how pleasant or unpleasant emotional stimuli were, and for depressive symptoms were mixed and depended on the type of sleep loss. The authors also detected nonlinear effects of the amount of sleep lost and differences depending on which stage of sleep was restricted, rapid eye movement sleep or slow-wave sleep. They conclude that extended wakefulness, shortened sleep and night-time awakenings adversely influence emotional functioning.

Tomaso, Johnson and Nelson (2021) ran three related meta-analyses on 241 effect sizes from 64 studies, from early childhood to late adulthood. Sleep loss had a moderate effect on negative mood (g = 0.45), stronger in younger samples and larger for total deprivation than for restriction. It had a large effect on positive mood (g = -0.94). After correction for publication bias, there was a modest negative effect on emotion (g = -0.11), which did not depend on whether the emotional stimuli were positive or negative. Sleep restriction had a small negative effect on adaptive emotion regulation (g = -0.32) and no significant effect on maladaptive emotion regulation (g = 0.14). All studies of adaptive emotion regulation had been conducted with youth samples.

## Reading the pattern carefully

Three features of these results deserve attention because they depart from the simplest story.

**The most consistent change is less positive affect, not only more negative affect.** Both syntheses find consistent reductions in positive mood: large in one (g = -0.94) and small to large, depending on the type of sleep loss, in the other. Effects on negative affect are present in one synthesis and mixed in the other. A tired person may not feel dramatically worse so much as flatter, with less capacity for enjoyment and interest. That loss matters for regulation, because positive experiences are among the resources that help people recover from stress.

**"Reactivity" is not one thing.** Popular accounts often say that sleep loss makes people emotionally overreactive. Palmer and colleagues found that sleep loss *blunted* arousal responses to emotional stimuli, and Tomaso and colleagues found a small effect on emotional responses that did not depend on valence. At the same time, anxiety symptoms rose. These can coexist: a person can feel more anxious overall and respond with less differentiated arousal to specific stimuli in a laboratory task. The honest reading is that sleep loss disturbs emotional functioning in several ways, and that "more reactive" is too simple a summary.

**Regulation has been studied less than emotion.** Palmer and Alfano (2017) reviewed the field using the process model of emotion regulation (Gross, 1998) as an organising framework. They observed that most sleep-emotion research had focused on emotions generated as a consequence of inadequate sleep rather than on the regulatory processes that may alter those experiences, with constructs often poorly delineated. They found evidence of maladaptive changes at multiple stages of emotion generation and regulation and called for experiments that identify which points in the regulation process are most vulnerable to sleep loss. The small effect on adaptive regulation in the Tomaso meta-analysis, limited to young people, shows how thin the direct evidence on regulation still is.

## A lowered threshold for stress

Minkel and colleagues (2012) asked whether sleep loss simply amplifies stress responses or interacts with stressors in a more specific way. In two laboratory experiments with a total of 53 healthy adults, one group was kept awake for a night and the other slept. Participants then completed cognitive tasks under low-stress or high-stress conditions, manipulated through task difficulty, time pressure and feedback. After the low-stress condition, sleep-deprived participants reported greater subjective stress, anxiety and anger than rested controls. After the high-stress condition, negative mood and stress rose about equally in both groups.

The authors suggest that sleep deprivation lowers the psychological threshold at which cognitive demands are perceived as stressful, rather than selectively increasing the size of negative responses to severe demands. The study is small and concerns one night of total deprivation, but the pattern is plausible and practically important. After a short night, ordinary irritations that would otherwise pass may be experienced as stressful, and the anger they produce may be read as a sign that something must be said or done.

## Sleep and emotional memory: a proposed model

Walker and van der Helm (2009) reviewed findings from basic and clinical research and proposed a model in which sleep modulates affective neural systems overnight and reprocesses recent emotional experiences, which would help restore appropriate next-day reactivity. They proposed a specific hypothesis about rapid eye movement sleep and emotional memory processing, and suggested that it may offer insight into the association between sleep abnormalities and mood disturbances.

The model is influential and has stimulated much of the later experimental work. It is a hypothesis, not an established mechanism. The later meta-analytic finding that sleep loss blunts rather than heightens arousal responses to emotional stimuli, and that effects depend on the sleep stage restricted, is a reminder that the neural account remains under active test. Imaging findings in this area describe measured activity associated with sleep conditions; they do not show what a person feels ([Research note 05](@/research/measuring-emotion/index.md)).

## Over months and years: insomnia and depression

Experiments last a night or a week. For longer time scales the evidence is observational. Baglioni and colleagues (2011) meta-analysed 21 longitudinal epidemiological studies that measured insomnia complaints at baseline and depression at follow-up. Using a random-effects model, the overall odds ratio for insomnia predicting depression was 2.60 (95% CI 1.98 to 3.42). When outliers were removed, the studies were no longer heterogeneous and the fixed-effects odds ratio was 2.10 (95% CI 1.86 to 2.38). The authors summarise this as non-depressed people with insomnia having about twice the risk of developing depression compared with people without sleep difficulties. They note that the included studies did not always consider other intervening variables.

An association of this kind cannot show that insomnia causes depression. Insomnia can be an early sign of a developing depressive episode, and both can share causes such as chronic stress. What the finding does show is that persistent poor sleep is not a trivial companion of distress.

## Trials that improved sleep

A stronger test of the causal question comes from randomised trials. Scott and colleagues (2021) meta-analysed 65 randomised controlled trials, comprising 72 interventions and 8,608 participants, that reported the effects of an intervention that improved sleep on mental health. Improving sleep led to a medium effect on composite mental health (g+ = -0.53), depression (g+ = -0.63), anxiety (g+ = -0.51) and rumination (g+ = -0.49), a small-to-medium effect on stress (g+ = -0.42) and a small effect on positive psychosis symptoms (g+ = -0.26). Greater improvements in sleep quality led to greater improvements in mental health, a dose-response relationship. The authors interpret the findings as suggesting that sleep is causally related to the experience of mental health difficulties.

Two cautions apply. The meta-analysis selected trials in which the intervention improved sleep, which is appropriate for the question asked but means it does not estimate how often attempts to improve sleep succeed. And the abstract does not break the effects down by type of intervention, so the result supports the value of better sleep, not of any particular technique.

The effect on rumination is worth noting for this library. Repetitive negative thinking is one of the processes most consistently associated with prolonged distress ([Research note 15](@/research/rumination-and-repetitive-thought/index.md)), and lying awake is often where it happens.

## What about everyday sleep habits?

Advice on sleep usually takes the form of sleep hygiene: regular timing, limiting caffeine and alcohol, a quiet room, and so on. Irish and colleagues (2015) reviewed the empirical evidence for such recommendations with an emphasis on the general population. They note that sleep hygiene education has been ineffective as a treatment in clinical sleep medicine. Epidemiological and experimental research generally supported associations between individual recommendations and night-time sleep, but the direct effects of individual recommendations on sleep remained largely untested in the general population.

This is a useful correction to confident lists of sleep rules. The habits are reasonable, low-risk and associated with better sleep, and the evidence that following them improves sleep for a given person is weaker than their popularity suggests. Persistent insomnia is a different matter, for which structured treatments delivered by clinicians exist.

## Relevance to catharsis and regulation

The findings above suggest a practical inversion of the catharsis intuition. After an emotional strain, the impulse is often to process it immediately and intensely: to talk late into the night, to write until the early hours, or to replay the event until it feels settled. If that comes at the cost of sleep, the next day begins with less positive affect, more anxiety and a lower threshold for stress, conditions under which the strain is likely to feel larger, not smaller.

Protecting sleep does not resolve anything by itself. It preserves the capacity to regulate, to reappraise and to act the following day. The emotional experience that seems to demand release at midnight is not the same experience in the morning, and the evidence does not support the idea that it must be dealt with before sleeping.

## Practical implications, with limits

- **Treat sleep as part of recovery after a strain.** Where possible, keep the usual sleep window rather than using the night to process the event.
- **Expect a lower threshold after a short night.** Irritation and anxiety the next day may partly reflect sleep loss; postpone consequential conversations or decisions when possible.
- **If thoughts keep you awake, park them.** Writing down the concern and a concrete next step, and returning to it at a set time the next day, is consistent with evidence on repetitive thought, though it has not been tested as a sleep intervention here ([Advice](@/advice/interrupt-rumination-with-concrete-steps/index.md)).
- **Seek help for persistent insomnia.** Difficulty sleeping on most nights for weeks, especially with low mood or anxiety, warrants talking with a clinician; effective treatments exist.

These implications are reasoned from the evidence; they have not been tested as a package.

## Limits of the evidence

Experimental studies manipulate short periods of sleep loss in healthy volunteers, often in laboratories, and their emotional measures vary widely. Evidence on regulation processes specifically is limited, and one set of findings on adaptive regulation comes only from young people. Longitudinal associations between insomnia and depression cannot establish direction. The trial meta-analysis supports improved sleep in general rather than specific everyday habits, and the evidence for individual sleep hygiene recommendations in the general population is weak. The neural model of overnight emotional processing is a hypothesis. The [evidence ledger](@/evidence/index.md) records the grade and review date of each claim.
