## The hypothesis

The hydraulic model of emotion predicts that expressing anger discharges it. Hitting a punching bag, shouting into a pillow or playing an aggressive game should therefore lower anger and reduce later aggression. The advice has been repeated in popular psychology for decades, and it feels plausible because venting is often followed by a sense of release.

## The experiments

Bushman, Baumeister and Stack (1999) tested the idea directly. Participants read a message claiming that hitting a punching bag relieves anger, a message claiming the opposite, or a neutral message, and were then provoked. Those who read the pro-catharsis message were more inclined to choose the punching bag. In a follow-up, participants who had vented were subsequently more aggressive toward the person who had provoked them, not less. Belief in catharsis did not protect against aggression; it steered people toward a strategy that increased it.

Bushman (2002) then compared three responses to provocation. One group hit a punching bag while thinking about the person who had angered them (rumination). A second group hit the bag while thinking about getting physically fit (distraction). A control group sat quietly for the same time. The rumination group reported the most anger and behaved most aggressively. The control group, which did nothing, behaved least aggressively. Distraction fell in between.

## What the evidence does and does not show

The studies are laboratory experiments with brief provocations, mostly with university students, and aggression is measured with standardised tasks such as choosing the intensity of an unpleasant noise delivered to another participant. They show that venting anger does not reduce short-term aggression and can increase it. They do not show that every form of emotional expression is harmful, and they do not describe what happens over months.

The proposed mechanism is rehearsal. Venting while attention stays on the provocation keeps anger-related thoughts active and makes aggressive responses more accessible. Physical arousal alone is not release: exertion raises arousal, and if attention remains on the grievance, that arousal is experienced as anger.

## What reduces anger instead

Strategies that lower arousal, such as waiting, slow breathing or genuine distraction, let anger decline without rehearsing it. Cognitive reappraisal changes the interpretation of the provocation before or while the emotion unfolds; people who habitually use reappraisal report more positive and less negative emotion than people who habitually suppress (Gross, 1998; Gross & John, 2003). Reflecting on an upsetting experience from a self-distanced perspective, as an observer rather than reliving it, reduced emotional reactivity and rumination compared with immersed recall (Kross, Ayduk & Mischel, 2005).

These strategies share one feature. They change the relationship to the emotion rather than trying to empty it out.

## Relevance to the artifact

The process shown in the artifact, repetition, synchronization and discharge, is not venting anger at a target, and the findings above should not be stretched to cover dancing or music. Their relevance is narrower and still important: the feeling of release is not evidence that the cause of an emotion has been dealt with, and some kinds of release keep the emotion rehearsed. That is the distinction the next notes develop.
