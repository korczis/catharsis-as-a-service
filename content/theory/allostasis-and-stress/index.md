## What "the body changes state" can mean

Accounts of catharsis often have a bodily plot. Before, the chest is tight and the jaw clenched; during, there is shouting, crying or exertion; after, the body is loose and quiet. That description is phenomenologically accurate for many people. The question for this essay is what physiology says about such changes of state, and what it does not.

Stress physiology offers a vocabulary for this: *activation*, *mediators*, *reactivity*, *recovery*, and two concepts that attempt to connect short-term responses with long-term health, *allostasis* and *allostatic load*. Each has a defined meaning, a body of evidence and critics. This essay explains them in turn and then asks what they imply about relief and resolution.

## From homeostasis to allostasis

### Homeostasis

The classical idea of physiological regulation is homeostasis: internal variables such as temperature, blood glucose and blood pressure are kept within a range around a set point, and deviations are corrected by feedback. When something pushes a variable away from its set point, mechanisms detect the error and push it back.

### Allostasis as predictive regulation

Peter Sterling and Joseph Eyer introduced the term *allostasis* in a 1988 chapter on arousal pathology (Sterling & Eyer, 1988). The word is usually glossed as "stability through change". Sterling later set out the argument in detail (Sterling, 2012). The premise of the standard homeostatic model, he argued, is flawed: the goal of regulation is not to preserve constancy of the internal milieu but to continually adjust it to promote survival and reproduction. Feedback, which corrects errors after they occur, is inherently inefficient. Efficient regulation instead requires anticipating needs and preparing to satisfy them before they arise.

Sterling lists advantages of such predictive regulation: errors are reduced in magnitude and frequency; the capacities of different components are matched; resources are shared between systems; and errors are remembered and used to reduce future errors. In his account this strategy requires central coordination by the nervous system, which integrates many variables with prior knowledge to predict needs, set priorities and govern anticipatory behaviour, such as moving somewhere warmer before becoming cold.

The practical consequence of the idea is that set points are not fixed. Blood pressure rises in anticipation of standing up or of a demanding meeting, not only in response to a fall. Physiological change during emotional episodes is, on this view, largely anticipatory preparation for action ([allostasis](../../glossary/#allostasis)).

## Allostatic load

### The concept

McEwen and Stellar (1993) extended allostasis over time. Their article emphasises the hidden cost of chronic stress to the body over long periods, which acts as a predisposing factor for the effects of acute stressful events. They define [allostatic load](../../glossary/#allostatic-load) as the cost of chronic exposure to fluctuating or heightened neural or neuroendocrine response resulting from repeated or chronic environmental challenge that an individual reacts to as particularly stressful. The formulation stresses cascading relationships, beginning early in life, between environmental factors and genetic predispositions, which lead to large individual differences in susceptibility.

### Protective and damaging effects

A later review put the central idea in terms of mediators (McEwen, 1998). Stress mediators such as cortisol and catecholamines are protective in the short term: they mobilise energy, support cardiovascular responses and prepare the organism for demands. When they are chronically activated or poorly terminated, they contribute to damage. The same systems that help in an acute challenge can, when repeatedly or persistently engaged, contribute to wear and tear ([claim](@/research/stress-and-social-buffering/index.md)).

The reasoning here is important for catharsis. Allostatic load is not a quantity of stored stress waiting to be released. It is the cumulative physiological cost of how often, how strongly and for how long systems have been activated. Nothing in the concept suggests that a single intense discharge reduces it.

### Measuring load

To test the idea, researchers constructed indices that combine biomarkers from several systems, typically neuroendocrine, immune, metabolic and cardiovascular. A review of this work reported that numerous studies using such an index demonstrated greater prediction of morbidity and mortality beyond traditional detection methods used in biomedical practice (Juster, McEwen, & Lupien, 2010). The review also identifies risk and protective factors associated with increased load.

The site grades this with low confidence. The studies are observational; the composition of the index differs between studies, which makes comparison and synthesis difficult; the review is narrative rather than systematic; and prediction does not establish that the index measures a single underlying process.

## What happens during an acute stress response

A brief, deliberately general description helps to interpret claims about bodily "release". During an acute challenge, activity of the sympathetic branch of the [autonomic nervous system](../../glossary/#autonomic-nervous-system) typically increases, with effects on heart rate, blood pressure, breathing and sweating, while parasympathetic influence on the heart is reduced. The hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis responds more slowly, with cortisol rising later than the autonomic response and remaining elevated after it. When the challenge ends, these systems return towards baseline at different speeds.

Three cautions follow from the evidence base of this site.

1. **These responses are not emotion-specific.** A meta-analysis of 202 studies found that autonomic responses did not clearly distinguish one emotion category from another and varied substantially within categories (Siegel et al., 2018). Exertion, anticipation, excitement and anger can all raise heart rate ([Theory 04](@/theory/basic-and-constructed-emotion/index.md)).
2. **A return to baseline is not the removal of a cause.** Physiological variables return towards baseline after most challenges whether or not the challenge has been resolved.
3. **Timing differs across systems.** A person can feel calm while a slower system is still elevated, or feel unsettled after heart rate has normalised.

## Recovery: the neglected half

If allostatic load reflects how long activation lasts, then recovery should matter as much as reactivity. Linden, Earle, Gerin and Christenfeld (1997) examined this. Of 105 studies of physiological reactivity published in four major journals over two years, 63% had collected recovery data, but only 23% reported them. The authors concluded that recovery had been neglected, argued for its conceptual and ecological importance, and noted that studies using recovery protocols revealed findings that were not apparent from reactivity comparisons alone.

The point is methodological but has a direct bearing on catharsis. A cathartic episode is usually described in terms of recovery: the state after. Physiologically, recovery can be fast or slow, complete or incomplete, and it can be interrupted by renewed activation. How recovery unfolds after an emotional episode is a separate question from how intense the episode was.

## Perseverative cognition

Why would activation persist after a stressor has ended? Brosschot, Gerin and Thayer (2006) proposed the *perseverative cognition hypothesis*. Worry and rumination are common responses to stress, yet biopsychological models had largely focused on activation during stressors. The authors argued that perseverative cognition prolongs stress-related affective and physiological activation, both in anticipation of stressors and after them. They reviewed evidence that worry, rumination and anticipatory stress are associated with enhanced cardiovascular, endocrine, immune and neurovisceral activity, and described the findings as preliminary support ([perseverative cognition](../../glossary/#perseverative-cognition)).

This connects stress physiology with emotion regulation. A person who repeatedly rehearses a conflict, whether alone or while venting, may extend the physiological episode as well as the emotional one. The association is not evidence that rumination causes disease, and the authors are explicit about the preliminary status of their hypothesis. It is, however, a plausible route by which the way an emotion is handled could affect the duration of activation. See also [rumination](../../glossary/#rumination).

## Social context

Physiological stress does not occur in isolation. The perceived availability of support has been found to buffer the effects of stress, while social integration has a main effect on wellbeing, largely in observational studies (Cohen & Wills, 1985). The [Research note on stress and social buffering](@/research/stress-and-social-buffering/index.md) discusses this. For allostatic load, the relevant implication is that the frequency and duration of activation depend on circumstances and relationships as well as on individual responses.

## The critique: does allostasis add anything?

The allostasis framework is widely used but not universally accepted. Day (2005) offers a direct critique. Examining the arguments for supplementing homeostasis with allostasis and allostatic load, he concludes that the concept of allostasis was introduced largely through misunderstandings of homeostasis; that, in terms of understanding how organisms operate, it is not clear that allostasis or allostatic load offer anything not already apparent or derivable from an accurate reading of homeostasis; and that attempts to subsume stress under allostasis collapse the study of homeostatic responses and stress responses together, when there is evidence for pathways that merit the title of stress circuitry.

The site records this critique as a contested position. Its force is conceptual. It does not deny that chronic stress has costs, or that anticipatory regulation occurs. It questions whether new terms are needed, and whether they clarify or blur the concept of stress. Readers should treat "allostatic load" as a useful organising idea whose boundaries are disputed, not as a measured quantity.

## Allostasis, catharsis and resolution

What does this body of theory imply for the idea that catharsis releases stress? The following is interpretation, drawing on the evidence above.

**Release is not the same as recovery.** Many cathartic activities, such as shouting, hitting, crying intensely or exhausting exercise, increase physiological activation before it falls. The felt relief afterwards coincides with a return towards baseline. That return would occur in any case once activation stops. The meta-analytic finding that arousal-decreasing activities reduced anger while arousal-increasing activities were not effective overall is consistent with this reading ([Research note 02](@/research/venting-hypothesis/index.md)).

**Load is cumulative, not stored.** On the allostatic load model, what matters for long-term cost is repeated and prolonged activation. An episode of release does not subtract from load; if it adds another bout of activation, or is followed by rumination, it may extend it. There is insufficient evidence to quantify this for any particular activity.

**Resolution acts on exposure and duration.** If a stressor recurs, or if it is repeatedly rehearsed in thought, activation recurs. Changes that reduce recurrence (changing a situation), shorten activation (effective regulation, support) or reduce perseverative cognition act on the variables the model identifies. Relief that leaves those unchanged leaves the pattern of activation unchanged.

**Feeling calm is informative but limited.** A subjective sense of calm after an episode is associated with physiological recovery in many situations, but systems recover at different speeds, and self-report is not a measure of load.

## Clinical boundary

Persistent physical symptoms, sleep problems, or distress that does not ease are reasons to consult a medical or mental health professional. Concepts such as allostatic load are research constructs; they are not clinical tests, and nothing on this site assesses any reader's stress physiology.

## What this essay concludes

Stress physiology gives a precise sense to the idea that the body changes state. Activation is anticipatory and protective in the short term; its costs accumulate with repetition and duration; recovery is a distinct and often neglected process; and worry and rumination are associated with activation that outlasts its cause. The allostasis framework organises these ideas, and it is contested as adding little to homeostasis. None of it supports a picture of stress as a stored quantity released by an intense episode. On this account, what shapes long-term physiological cost is how often and how long systems are engaged, which is why the question of what changed after relief matters physiologically as well as psychologically.
