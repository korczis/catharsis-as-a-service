## Why it works

There are two ways of thinking about a painful memory. From an *immersed* perspective, a person re-enters the scene and re-experiences it. From a *distanced* perspective, the person views the scene from outside. Both involve thinking about the same event, but they have different consequences.

## What the evidence shows

Kross, Ayduk and Mischel (2005) asked participants to recall an anger-provoking experience and to analyse their feelings from either an immersed or a distanced perspective. Distanced analysis reduced emotional reactivity and the tendency to recount the event in a rehearsing way, while still allowing participants to understand why they had felt as they did. Kross and Ayduk (2011) reviewed later studies that replicated and extended these effects to other negative emotions and outcomes.

The finding fits the distinction drawn by Watkins (2008) between unconstructive and constructive repetitive thought. Abstract, immersed brooding tends to maintain distress, while reflective, concrete processing can support recovery.

## What further research adds

Two meta-analyses tested manipulations of psychological distance (k = 230) and of level of abstraction (k = 98) on emotional experience (Moran & Eyal, 2022). A distant perspective attenuated emotional experience (g = 0.52), with weaker effects for high-level emotions such as self-conscious emotions (g = 0.29) than for low-level emotions such as basic emotions (g = 0.64). Abstraction attenuated only low-level emotions (g = 0.2) and showed a reverse, non-significant effect for high-level emotions. Distance and abstraction are therefore not interchangeable.

Language offers a simple route to distance. Across seven studies with 585 participants, using non-first-person pronouns and one's own name during introspection enhanced self-distancing (Kross et al., 2014). Under social stress, people who used this kind of self-talk performed better according to objective raters, displayed less distress and engaged in less maladaptive post-event processing, and none of these findings was moderated by trait social anxiety. A review of distancing research notes that its neurocognitive mechanisms remain unclear and that inconsistent terminology and methods make the literature difficult to synthesise (Powers & LaBar, 2019).

## In short

Returning to a painful experience is not the problem. Re-living it is. Viewing it from a distance keeps the benefit of reflection without the cost of re-experiencing.
