## Why it can help

Norton and Gino (2014) found in three experiments that people who reflected on rituals they had performed after past losses, or who completed a novel ritual after a loss, reported lower grief. The link was mediated by increased feelings of control, and it held for people who did not believe in rituals as well as for those who did. A loss removes control over something that mattered; a ritual, with its fixed sequence, gives back a small, deliberate act.

Hobson and colleagues (2018) describe rituals as regulating emotions, goal states and social connection, partly through their physical structure and partly through their meaning. A mourning ritual can do several things at once: mark that something has ended, gather people, and give a form to a relationship that continues in memory.

## Why it need not be cathartic

The supported mechanism is control and structure, not discharge. The dual process model of bereavement (Stroebe & Schut, 1999) describes adaptive coping as an oscillation between confronting the loss and attending to ordinary life, including respite from grieving. That is why the steps above include stepping away afterwards. It is also why there is no reason to judge a ritual by how much emotion it produces.

## What the evidence does not show

A Dutch questionnaire study found that bereaved people valued funerals and rituals, yet their evaluations and use of rituals were not significantly associated with grief reactions six months and three years later (Mitima-Verloop et al., 2021). Rituals can be experienced as helpful without measurably changing the course of grief. Most people adjust to bereavement without professional intervention (Stroebe, Schut & Stroebe, 2007), and psychological interventions show larger effects in trials that enrolled people with high grief symptoms and in trials of people at least six months after the loss (Johannsen et al., 2019). The evidence and the clinical boundary are set out in [Research note 11](@/research/grief-and-rituals/index.md).
