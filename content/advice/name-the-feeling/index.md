## Why it works

Naming an emotion shifts processing from an immediate, bodily response toward a verbal, conceptual one. In the process model of emotion regulation, this changes how the situation and the response are represented, which can alter the emotion's intensity and course (Gross, 1998).

## What the evidence shows

Lieberman and colleagues (2007) asked participants to label the emotion shown in photographs of faces, to label another attribute such as gender, or simply to look. Labelling the emotion was associated with lower amygdala activity and higher activity in the right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, and the two were inversely related. The authors describe affect labelling as a form of implicit emotion regulation: it helps even though people do not intend to regulate.

Naming also makes distance possible. Kross and Ayduk (2011) reviewed evidence that reflecting on negative experiences from a self-distanced perspective helps people make meaning of them without being overwhelmed. A precise label is often the first step in taking that perspective.

## What further research adds

The effect of labelling is not limited to brain imaging. In four studies, self-reported distress was lower while participants labelled the emotion in negative pictures than while they passively watched them, and the reduction was similar to that produced by reappraisal and by distraction (Lieberman et al., 2011). Participants nonetheless predicted that labelling would increase their distress, even after experiencing the opposite. With positive pictures, labelling was associated with less self-reported pleasure, which suggests that it dampens affective responses in general rather than only relieving negative feelings. Torre and Lieberman (2018) review this work as evidence that affect labelling is a form of implicit emotion regulation that may not feel like regulation while it happens.

In a clinical application, spider-fearful people underwent a brief course of exposure to a live spider (Kircanski, Lieberman & Craske, 2012). One week later, with a different spider in another context, the group that had labelled their feelings during exposure showed a smaller skin conductance response than the groups that had reappraised, distracted themselves or received exposure alone, but it did not differ from them in self-reported fear. Greater use of anxiety and fear words during exposure was associated with greater reductions in fear responding.

## In short

Before trying to release a feeling, give it a name. It costs a few seconds, requires no discharge, and changes what happens next.
