## Why it matters

Stress responses protect the body in the short term and contribute to wear when they are activated too often or do not switch off (McEwen, 1998). Anything that reduces how strongly or how long stress responses are engaged has long-term value.

## What the evidence shows

Cohen and Wills (1985) reviewed studies of social support and well-being. Social integration had a general positive association with health, and perceived availability of support protected people specifically against the effects of stressful events. The buffering effect was strongest when the kind of support available matched the demands of the stressor.

Rimé (2009) reviewed research showing that people share most of their emotional experiences with others. Sharing reliably strengthens social bonds and brings attention and empathy, even though, on its own, it does not reduce the emotional impact of a memory. Sharing therefore contributes to support indirectly, by maintaining the relationships that make support available.

## Relation to collective experiences

A crowd can create a strong feeling of belonging for one night. Buffering depends on relationships that persist into the following week, when a specific stressor arrives. Both are valuable. Only the second is available on demand.
