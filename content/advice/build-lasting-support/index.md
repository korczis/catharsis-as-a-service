## Why it matters

Stress responses protect the body in the short term and contribute to wear when they are activated too often or do not switch off (McEwen, 1998). Anything that reduces how strongly or how long stress responses are engaged has long-term value.

## What the evidence shows

Cohen and Wills (1985) reviewed studies of social support and well-being. Social integration had a general positive association with health, and perceived availability of support protected people specifically against the effects of stressful events. The buffering effect was strongest when the kind of support available matched the demands of the stressor.

Rimé (2009) reviewed research showing that people share most of their emotional experiences with others. Sharing reliably strengthens social bonds and brings attention and empathy, even though, on its own, it does not reduce the emotional impact of a memory. Sharing therefore contributes to support indirectly, by maintaining the relationships that make support available.

## What further research adds

The long-term stakes are large. A meta-analysis of 148 studies with 308,849 participants found that people with stronger social relationships had a 50% increased likelihood of survival (odds ratio 1.50), a result that was consistent across age, sex, initial health status, cause of death and follow-up period (Holt-Lunstad, Smith & Layton, 2010). The association was strongest for complex measures of social integration and weakest for whether a person lived alone or with others. The authors judged the influence comparable with well-established risk factors for mortality. The estimate describes an association between relationships and survival, not a controlled test of building relationships.

How support is given also matters. Bolger, Zuckerman and Kessler (2000) note that although perceived availability of support buffers the effects of stressors, research had failed to show an association between actually receiving support and adjustment. In a daily diary study of couples, many support transactions reported by the supporter were not reported by the recipient, and these invisible transactions promoted adjustment to a major stressor. A review of 100 studies of social support interventions found some support for their overall usefulness, but not enough evidence to conclude which interventions work best for which problems (Hogan, Linden & Najarian, 2002).

## Relation to collective experiences

A crowd can create a strong feeling of belonging for one night. Buffering depends on relationships that persist into the following week, when a specific stressor arrives. Both are valuable. Only the second is available on demand.
