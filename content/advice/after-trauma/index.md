## Why early forced disclosure is not recommended

Psychological debriefing, a single session soon after a traumatic event in which people describe the event and express their reactions, was once widely used because it seemed consistent with the idea that early emotional release prevents later disorder. Rose, Bisson, Churchill and Wessely (2002) systematically reviewed randomised controlled trials of single-session individual debriefing. They found no evidence that it prevented post-traumatic stress disorder or reduced general distress, and in trials with longer follow-up there was some evidence of an increased risk of post-traumatic stress disorder among people who had been debriefed. The authors concluded that compulsory debriefing should cease.

## What effective treatment involves

Emotional processing theory (Foa & Kozak, 1986) explains why structure matters. Change requires activating the traumatic memory together with information that corrects its threatening meaning, repeated over time within a safe, guided process. Treatments derived from this principle, such as prolonged exposure, differ fundamentally from one-off emotional release: they are planned, repeated and focused on new learning.

Other structured approaches also have evidence. For example, mindfulness-based therapies moderately reduce symptoms of anxiety and depression, with larger effects in people with diagnosed anxiety and mood disorders (Hofmann et al., 2010). The right approach depends on clinical assessment.

## In short

After trauma, release is neither required nor sufficient. Time, safety, support and, when symptoms persist, structured professional treatment are what the evidence supports.
