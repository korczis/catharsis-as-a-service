## Why it works for mood

In studies of everyday life, physical activity consistently predicted higher positive affect over the following few hours (Liao, Shonkoff & Dunton, 2015). Over weeks, randomised trials pooled in meta-analyses associate physical activity with lower depressive and anxiety symptoms: a medium effect on depression and a small effect on anxiety in non-clinical adults (Rebar et al., 2015), moderate effects for walking or jogging, yoga and strength training in major depression (Noetel et al., 2024), and medium effects across populations in an umbrella review (Singh et al., 2023).

Intensity matters for how activity feels. Pleasure tends to decline above the ventilatory or lactate threshold, while most people experience pleasant changes below it, and self-selected intensity appears to foster tolerance of harder effort (Ekkekakis, Parfitt & Petruzzello, 2011). Activity that feels tolerable is more likely to be repeated, and repetition is where the symptom benefits in trials came from.

## Why it does not work for anger

Across 154 studies, arousal-decreasing activities reduced anger and aggression (g = -0.63), while arousal-increasing activities such as hitting a bag, jogging or cycling were ineffective overall (g = -0.02) (Kjærvik & Bushman, 2024). In an earlier experiment, hitting a punching bag while thinking about the provocateur produced the most anger and aggression (Bushman, 2002). Exertion raises arousal; if attention stays on the grievance, that arousal does not drain away.

The two findings are compatible because they measure different outcomes. Activity can lift energetic, positive feeling and still leave anger where it was. The reasoning is set out in [Research note 12](@/research/exercise-and-mood/index.md) and [Research note 02](@/research/venting-hypothesis/index.md).
